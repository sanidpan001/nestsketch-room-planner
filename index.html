What Is a Room Layout Planner?

A room layout planner is a scale-drawing tool that lets you arrange furniture in a room before you lift a single heavy object. Instead of guessing whether the new sectional will fit, you draw your room's exact dimensions, drop in furniture blocks sized to real-world measurements, and shuffle them around until the plan works. NestSketch takes the idea further: it is a furniture arrangement tool with a built-in second opinion. As you drag pieces across the blueprint grid, a rule-based mistake detector watches for the errors real people make every day — blocked walkways, televisions parked too close to the sofa, dining chairs with nowhere to pull out, and door swings that smack into bookshelves.

Everything runs in your browser. There is no account to create, no trial to expire, and no watermark stamped on your plan. When the layout feels right, you can export it as a PNG and take it to the store, send it to a partner, or hand it to a mover. Think of it as graph paper that does the math for you.

Will My Sofa Fit? Measure Before You Buy

The most searched question in furniture planning is also the simplest: will my sofa fit? The answer has two parts. First, the sofa must fit through your doors, hallways, and stairwells on delivery day — measure the narrowest point of that route, not just the room. A 7-foot sofa will not turn a 3-foot hallway corner, no matter how spacious the living room is. Second, the sofa must fit the room with its clearances intact. A sofa needs roughly 18 inches of walkway in front of it for a coffee table zone, or 30 to 36 inches if people walk past it regularly.

In NestSketch, every furniture block carries realistic default dimensions: a standard three-seat sofa is 7 by 3 feet, a loveseat is 5 by 2.8 feet, a queen bed is 5 by 6.7 feet. Select any block to type in the exact measurements from the product page you are considering. If the piece hangs over the room boundary, the planner clamps it back inside and tells you it does not fit. That is your will-my-sofa-fit calculator, answered visually instead of with a tape measure and wishful thinking.

One more tip from delivery drivers: measure diagonally. A sofa's diagonal depth is often smaller than its width, which is why some pieces fit through a doorway when tilted even though the width suggests otherwise. Plan the room first, then plan the route in.

The Walkway Rules Every Room Must Follow

Interior designers return to the same handful of clearance numbers because human bodies do not change size. The main walkway through a room — the path from the door to the seating area, or from the kitchen to the dining table — needs 30 to 36 inches of clear width. That is enough for one person to walk comfortably and for two people to pass without turning sideways. Secondary paths, like the gap between a bed and a wall you only use on one side, can shrink to 24 inches. Anything under 18 inches is not a walkway; it is a squeeze.

This is where most DIY layouts fail. People push furniture against walls to "open up" the room, then discover the only route to the window threads between the coffee table and the TV stand with inches to spare. NestSketch checks every pair of furniture pieces and flags gaps under 18 inches as tight passages, explaining in plain English which two pieces are the problem and how much space a comfortable lane needs. It also guards the entry: a 3.5-foot-wide corridor running 4 feet in from each door must stay clear, because the first thing you do in any room is walk into it.

Turn on the walkway heatmap to see these rules as color. The planner computes, for every half-foot square of your floor, how far it is from the nearest furniture. Squares inside furniture or pressed against it glow red; squares with three or more feet of breathing room fade away. A good plan shows cool, open lanes connecting the door to every seat and the table.

Sofa-to-TV Distance: The 6-to-8-Foot Rule

Sit too close to a big screen and you will crane your neck and see pixels; sit too far and the movie feels distant. The widely used guideline is 1.5 to 2.5 times the screen's diagonal measurement. For the common 55-to-65-inch television, that lands at roughly 6 to 8 feet between the screen and your eyes — which, on a floor plan, means between the front of the TV console and the front edge of the sofa.

NestSketch measures that edge-to-edge distance automatically whenever your plan contains a sofa and a TV console. Drop below 6 feet and the mistake detector flags it with the exact current distance, so you can nudge the sofa back or reconsider the screen size. Small rooms sometimes force a compromise; knowing the number lets you compromise deliberately instead of discovering the problem after the wall mount is drilled in.

Dining Chairs Need 36 Inches Behind Them

A dining chair is not 20 inches deep — it is 20 inches deep plus the space a human needs to push back, stand, and walk away. That working depth is about 36 inches from the table's edge. Forget it and you get the classic dining-room comedy: guests wedged between the table and the sideboard, performing an awkward sideways shuffle every time someone needs the restroom.

The planner handles this with a directional check. When a chair sits tucked against a table, NestSketch projects a 3-foot pull-out zone behind the chair — on the side facing away from the table — and looks for walls or furniture inside it. If the sideboard blocks the zone, or the wall is too close, you get a plain-English warning naming the blocker. Slide the table toward the room's center or choose a narrower sideboard, and the warning clears.

Door Swings: The Forgotten Collision Zone

Every hinged door sweeps a quarter-circle with a radius equal to the door's width — typically 3 feet. Furniture placed inside that arc gets hit, blocks the door, or forces you to squeeze through a half-open doorway for the life of the room. It is one of the most common layout mistakes because the arc is invisible once the door is open against the wall.

Add your doors in the room setup panel — pick the wall, slide the position, set the width — and NestSketch draws the swing arc as a dashed red quarter-circle on the blueprint. Any furniture overlapping the arc triggers a high-severity warning. Pocket doors and outward-swinging doors are exempt: set the swing to "out" and the check is skipped, which is itself a useful design trick if a swing keeps colliding with pieces you love.

What Size Rug Should You Buy?

Rug sizing has a simple professional rule: the rug should be large enough that the front legs of every seat in the arrangement sit on it, with about 18 inches of rug extending beyond the furniture on each side. A rug that is too small looks like a postage stamp floating under the coffee table; it visually shrinks the room instead of anchoring it. In a living room with a 7-foot sofa and two chairs, that rule usually points to an 8-by-10-foot rug, and larger seating groups step up to 9-by-12.

NestSketch computes this from your actual plan. It takes the bounding box of your seating zone — sofa, chairs, coffee table — adds 18 inches per side, and recommends the smallest standard rug size (5x7, 6x9, 8x10, 9x12, or 10x14) that covers it and fits your room. One tap adds the recommended rug to the plan so you can see it in place.

If your plan already contains a rug, the recommender grades it: either confirming it is a good size for the zone or telling you exactly which size to trade up to. Rug regret is one of the most common furniture returns; a thirty-second check here can save a two-hundred-dollar mistake.

What Is a Good Openness Score?

The openness score is the percentage of your floor area not covered by furniture. Rugs do not count — you walk on rugs, so they do not reduce usable floor. A score above 65 feels airy, with generous lanes between pieces. Between 45 and 65 is the comfortable middle most living rooms land in. Below 45 the room starts feeling full, and under 30 it is genuinely crowded: movement gets awkward no matter how nicely the pieces are arranged.

Use the score as a reality check when comparing two arrangements of the same room. If version A scores 58 and version B scores 41 with the same furniture, version B has pieces sprawled inefficiently — usually a sign that something belongs against a different wall or that one piece too many is in the mix.

Small Room Layout Tricks That Actually Work

Small rooms punish every wasted inch, so the standard advice is to reduce the number of pieces, not just their size. One 7-foot sofa beats a loveseat plus two bulky armchairs because it leaves longer uninterrupted walkways. Choose furniture with visible legs: seeing floor underneath a sofa makes a room read larger even when the footprint is identical. Round dining tables seat the same number of people as square ones with no sharp corners jutting into walkways.

On the plan, test the two classic small-room moves. First, float the sofa: pulling it even 6 inches off the wall can open a walkway behind it that transforms circulation. Second, merge zones — a desk behind the sofa can double as a console table, removing a whole piece from the footprint. Watch the openness score climb as you experiment, and let the heatmap confirm the lanes stay genuinely walkable.

Frequently asked questions

Will my sofa fit in my room?

Add a sofa block in NestSketch, select it, and type the exact width and depth from the product page. If the piece cannot fit inside your room's dimensions with its clearances, the planner clamps it at the boundary and the mistake detector tells you what is wrong. Remember to also measure your doorways and hallways for delivery day.

How much walkway clearance do I need between furniture?

Plan 30 to 36 inches for main walkways (door to sofa, kitchen to table), 24 inches for secondary paths, and never less than 18 inches anywhere people walk. NestSketch flags any gap under 18 inches as a tight passage and keeps a 3.5-foot corridor clear in front of every door.

What size rug should I buy for my living room?

Choose a rug large enough that the front legs of the sofa and all chairs sit on it, with about 18 inches of rug beyond the furniture on each side — usually 8x10 ft for a standard seating group. NestSketch's rug recommender computes this from your actual furniture footprint and picks the right standard size.

How far should my sofa be from the TV?

Keep 6 to 8 feet between the sofa and the screen for a 55 to 65-inch TV (roughly 1.5 to 2.5 times the screen diagonal). The mistake detector measures your plan's edge-to-edge sofa-to-TV distance and warns you if it drops under 6 feet.

How much space do dining chairs need behind them?

Allow 36 inches from the table's edge so people can push back, stand, and walk away. NestSketch projects a 3-foot pull-out zone behind each tucked-in chair and flags walls or furniture that intrude into it.
